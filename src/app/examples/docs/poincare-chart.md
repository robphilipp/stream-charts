### the poincare chart

A Poincare chart (named for Henri Poincaré, who was thinking about planetary orbits at the time) plots a time-series against a delayed copy of itself. Each point is `(x[n], x[n+lag])` – a value on the x-axis, and the value `lag` steps later on the y-axis.

Why would you want that? Because it shows you structure that a plain time-series hides. Random noise fills the plot with a shapeless cloud. But a series generated by a rule – even a chaotic one that *looks* random in time – traces out a distinct shape. That makes the Poincare chart handy for spotting auto-correlations, and for telling "random" apart from "complicated, but deterministic."

#### what you're looking at

The example generates its time-series by *iterating* a function: start with some value `x`, compute `f(x)`, then `f(f(x))`, and so on. The example offers three classic functions, each with a parameter or two you can change:

- **Tent Map** – `f(x) = µ · min(x, 1 - x)`, with `µ` between 0 and 2. With a lag of 1, every point lands on the graph of the map itself – the tent it's named for. Try a lag of 2 and the tent splits in two.
- **Logistic Map** – `f(x) = r · x · (1 - x)`, with `r` between 0 and 4. The classic example of a simple rule that becomes chaotic: try `r = 3.2`, then `3.5`, then `4.0`.
- **Gauss Map** – `f(x) = exp(-α x²) + β`. Unlike the other two, its values range over `[-1, 1]`, and the axes change to match when you select it.

As the iterates stream in, the chart plots each one against the iterate `lag` steps later, and connects the points with a line (so you can watch the orbit hop around).

The chart has a tracker on *both* axes, so with the tracker on, you get a crosshair with the x and y values.

#### controls just for this chart

- **lag drop-down** – The lag, from 1 to 5. The y-axis label changes to match (`f[n+lag](x)`). Disabled while the chart is running.
- **iterate-function drop-down** – Selects the Tent, Logistic, or Gauss map. Selecting a different function clears the chart, since the old points have nothing to do with the new function. Disabled while the chart is running.
- **function parameters** – The fields next to the drop-down (`µ`, `r`, or `α` and `β`) set the selected function's parameters.
- **interpolation drop-down** – How the line gets from one point to the next, just like the scatter chart's. "No Line" leaves only the points, which shows the structure most clearly.
- **colorize lines** – Colors the line by age: it starts (at the oldest point) in the **from** color and sweeps through the colors of the spectrum to the **to** color (at the newest point), so you can follow the orbit's path – and see where it's been lately. Pick the two colors with the swatches beside the checkbox. Its header icon shows the line in your colors when it's on. *Wired to:* the plot's `colorizeLines`, `lineStartColor`, and `lineEndColor` properties.

The function, its parameters, and the lag are all picked up when you click **Run** – the example hands the data source a new generator for them each time it starts. So pause, change them, and run again. The iteration carries on from where it left off, using the new settings.

You'll notice there's no **Number of Series**, **Update Rate**, or **Buffering** here: the example plots a single series of iterates.

#### where the code lives

The example (yours to copy, change, or ignore):
- `src/app/examples/StreamingPoincareChart.tsx` – the control bar, the function parameter inputs, and the chart itself
- `src/app/examples/appstate/poincareChartStore.ts` – the example's state, including its data source
- `src/app/examples/dataproviders/randomIterateData.ts` – the iterate functions and their data generator

The library (what you'd use in your own application):
- `<Chart>`, `<ContinuousAxis>`, `<PoincarePlot>`, `<Tracker>` (two of them), `<Tooltip>` with `<PoincarePlotTooltipContent>`
- `IteratesDataSource` – holds the iterates
- `iteratesObservable` – turns a stream of values into the `(x[n], x[n+lag])` pairs the plot needs. The example's generator (`iteratesGenerator`, in the store) wraps its stream of iterates with it, which makes it a nice example of where the line falls: *what* the data is belongs to the example, *how* it becomes plottable belongs to the library.
